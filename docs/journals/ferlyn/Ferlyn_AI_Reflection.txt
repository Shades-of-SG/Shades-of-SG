AI REFLECTION – SHADES OF SG

Name: Ng Xin Yi Ferlyn
Project: Shades of SG
AI tools used: Codex and ChatGPT

Introduction

Throughout the Shades of SG project, I used AI as a development assistant across design, coding, testing, debugging, deployment, and documentation. My main use of AI was not to generate an entire application from a single prompt. Instead, I used it iteratively: I described a specific problem, reviewed the proposed solution or implementation, tested it, and then supplied more context or requested changes when the result did not meet the project’s functional, visual, security, or usability requirements.

This approach was especially important because Shades of SG is a full-stack application with connected public, registered-user, creator, and administrator journeys. A change that appeared correct on one page could affect authentication, database relationships, API behaviour, moderation rules, or another team member’s feature. AI helped me work across these layers more efficiently, but I remained responsible for deciding what the application should do and whether the generated work was suitable for our users and client.

Where AI Added Genuine Value

The greatest value AI provided was helping me translate detailed feature ideas into connected full-stack implementations. For example, the Reflection Wall began as a visually duplicated page with hardcoded or browser-stored data. I used Codex to inspect the component hierarchy, identify the duplicated layout and background wrappers, and connect the feature to Express, Sequelize, and the database. AI accelerated the creation of the API service, CRUD routes, authentication checks, ownership restrictions, loading and error states, and relevant tests. This allowed me to concentrate more on the user experience and the feature’s purpose as a community memory board.

AI also helped with features that required many edge cases to work together. In the Rhythm Game, guests needed to be able to play without creating an account, while authenticated users should be able to save their results. Codex helped implement a pending-score claim flow using session storage, an expiry period, a unique claim identifier, protected-route restoration, and duplicate protection. It also helped trace an issue where a newly registered user could lose the claim intent during email verification. The final solution preserved the intended action through registration, OTP verification, and automatic login. Without AI assistance, following the state across these pages and services would have taken substantially longer.

Another area where AI was valuable was defensive engineering. It helped identify that invalid song identifiers could reach PostgreSQL UUID fields and cause a 22P02 database error. From this diagnosis, reusable UUID validation middleware was introduced for affected song, beatmap, and analytics routes. AI also supported validation for profile biographies and interest tags, comment length limits, profanity filtering, comment rate limits, and database-backed duplicate prevention for likes. These were not simply interface improvements; they reduced invalid input, protected data integrity, and made failure states more predictable.

Testing was also made more systematic through AI assistance. I used Codex to generate and refine targeted frontend and backend tests for rhythm score claims, reflection discussions, creator access separation, profile behaviour, admin analytics, content moderation, and safety actions. It was useful for interpreting test failures and separating failures caused by new code from existing baseline problems, such as authentication mocks or stale storage fixtures. However, I did not treat a generated test or a passing targeted suite as proof that the entire application was correct. I also ran broader lint, build, and test commands, reviewed browser behaviour, and checked interactions across related routes.

AI added value during deployment troubleshooting as well. The YouTube transcription and extraction workflow worked locally but failed when deployed because the local Windows yt-dlp path and environment were not available on Vercel. AI helped explain the difference between a local executable path, frontend hosting, and a backend runtime such as Render. This improved my understanding that deployment problems cannot always be solved by copying local environment variables; the executable, operating system, runtime dependencies, network constraints, and server architecture must all be considered.

Where I Rejected or Significantly Modified AI Suggestions

I frequently changed AI-generated visual and interaction designs because a technically functional result did not always communicate the intended identity of Shades of SG. For the Reflection Wall, an early direction felt like a generic social-media feed or dashboard. I rejected equal rectangular cards and a tall landing-style hero because users should enter directly into an active memory board. I directed the design towards pinned memory notes with varied stationery details, a wider masonry layout, and a dark-purple background consistent with the rest of the application. I also changed the placement of the Add Reflection action: it belongs in the empty state when no memories exist, but moves to the toolbar once the board contains posts. These decisions were based on the feature’s purpose and visual hierarchy rather than on what was easiest to generate.

I also modified the authentication behaviour proposed during protected actions. A basic solution would redirect a guest to login and then return them to a page. That was insufficient because the user’s unfinished reflection draft or rhythm result could be lost. I required the implementation to preserve the exact intent and relevant temporary data across login, registration, and OTP verification, reopen or continue the correct action afterwards, handle expiry, and prevent duplicate score claims. This added complexity, but it reduced user frustration and made the guest-to-member journey coherent.

For community interactions, I did not accept a simple unrestricted comment and like system. I required comments to retain timestamps, support deletion by the comment owner, reflection creator, or administrator, and include both client-side and server-side validation. I also required conservative profanity filtering, rate limiting, anonymous privacy, and idempotent likes. The moderation design was further expanded so administrators could inspect unresolved reports, view the original reflection and comments, record reasons for decisions, issue warnings, and apply different restrictions to member and creator access. These modifications were necessary because community features affect safety, accountability, and privacy, not only engagement.

I also rejected or narrowed features when they no longer supported the final product. Trivia and the Instrument Playground were considered during earlier development, but they were later removed from the required navigation and page scope because they were no longer needed for the final experience. Similarly, I questioned whether a separate admin page was necessary before accepting additional administrative scope. This reminded me that AI can make it easy to add features, but more functionality is not automatically better. Each feature creates additional routes, permissions, tests, maintenance work, and possible confusion for users.

Human Oversight and Verification

My review process combined code inspection, screenshots, browser testing, automated tests, and repeated prompts. Screenshots were particularly useful for identifying problems that unit tests would not reveal, such as overlapping rhythm-game elements, a search icon appearing on a different line from its text, action buttons being poorly aligned, unreadable lane labels, or layouts feeling too layered. I used these observations to give precise follow-up instructions rather than accepting the first output.

I also reviewed whether generated code matched the existing project instead of assuming the suggested structure was correct. Where AI produced framework-specific or generic markup, I required it to be adapted to our React and Express architecture, shared components, service conventions, authentication context, database models, and route patterns. When branch integrations produced duplicate exports, malformed CSS, missing dependencies, or test instability, I used AI to diagnose the problems but still checked the affected files and commands before treating them as resolved.

An important lesson was that AI can sound confident even when it lacks full project context. A proposed change may work in isolation while breaking another team member’s route, mock, database assumption, or deployment environment. I therefore learned to provide concrete context such as current file behaviour, relevant API paths, screenshots, error output, user roles, and constraints. I also learned to ask AI to inspect existing code before modifying it and to request targeted tests for the changed behaviour. This produced better results than broad prompts such as asking it to “fix the page.”

What I Learned

Using AI improved both my productivity and my ability to reason about full-stack systems. I became more aware of how frontend state, API validation, authentication, database constraints, and deployment environments affect one another. The score-claim and reflection-discussion features showed me that the difficult part of development is often not the happy path, but preserving user intent and data integrity when users are guests, sessions expire, requests are repeated, input is invalid, or permissions differ.

I also learned that good prompting is a form of requirements analysis. My strongest results came from specifying the actor, expected flow, edge cases, constraints, existing architecture, and acceptance criteria. When the AI output was weak, the cause was often missing context or an unresolved product decision. Reviewing the output forced me to clarify those decisions and explain why one interaction or architecture was more appropriate than another.

At the same time, the project reinforced that AI does not replace human responsibility. I remained accountable for protecting anonymous users, choosing moderation rules, deciding what belonged in the final scope, reviewing visual consistency, checking tests, and ensuring that documentation reflected the final implementation. I did not accept suggestions merely because they were generated quickly or appeared technically sophisticated.

Conclusion

AI genuinely added value to Shades of SG by accelerating implementation, helping trace cross-layer bugs, improving test coverage, and supporting documentation across the development lifecycle. Its greatest benefit was giving me a faster way to explore and refine solutions. However, the quality of the final work depended on my review, domain understanding, and willingness to reject or substantially modify suggestions.

The project changed how I view AI-assisted software development. I now see AI as a capable collaborator that can propose, implement, and explain, but not as the owner of product decisions or quality assurance. Effective use requires precise context, iterative feedback, verification, and clear human judgment. My final contributions were therefore not simply AI-generated features; they were solutions shaped through repeated evaluation against the needs of Shades of SG, its users, the client, and the technical constraints of the full-stack system.
