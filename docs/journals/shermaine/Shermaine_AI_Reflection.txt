C2 — AI Reflection (Shermaine)

Throughout the development of the project, I used AI primarily as a development partner rather than as a replacement for my own decision-making. My use of AI evolved from generating and implementing features to helping me debug issues, evaluate alternative approaches and structure more complex technical work. Looking back across the development process, I found that AI added the most value when I already had a clear understanding of the problem but needed help translating that idea into an efficient implementation. At the same time, I learned that AI-generated suggestions were not always appropriate for the project's existing architecture, user experience or requirements. I therefore had to evaluate, modify and sometimes reject its suggestions rather than accepting them automatically.

One of the most valuable uses of AI was accelerating implementation while allowing me to iterate quickly. For example, during the development of the Learning Hub and Guided Music Lessons, I could describe the intended user flow and have AI help translate it into components, hooks, data structures and styling. This was particularly useful for larger features where implementing every part manually would have taken considerably longer. AI was also useful for identifying files and existing code that were relevant to a feature, allowing me to work within the existing codebase instead of rebuilding functionality unnecessarily.

However, I found that AI was most effective when I provided specific constraints rather than asking it to design everything independently. For larger features, I used clarification and planning before allowing implementation to proceed. For example, the Badge & Keepsake Journal required decisions about whether mock data or real backend tracking should be used, how badges should be structured and what should count as completion. Rather than allowing AI to make these decisions arbitrarily, I clarified the requirements and approved the implementation plan before coding began. This helped me realise that the quality of AI output depends heavily on how well I understand and communicate the actual problem.

A particularly important part of my learning was rejecting or significantly modifying AI suggestions when they conflicted with my intended user experience. One example was the Guided Music Lessons feature. AI initially proposed a direction based on the existing call-and-response practice mechanic, but I chose a playback-style teaching flow with Play, Pause, Restart and progress tracking instead. I also explicitly chose to migrate the existing songs into a common data structure rather than maintaining two separate lesson systems. This was not simply a technical preference; I considered what would make the learning experience more coherent and extensible. I also specified that the existing rhythm game should not be affected by the changes. This demonstrated to me that AI could suggest technically viable solutions, but I still needed to judge whether those solutions were appropriate for the overall product.

I encountered a similar situation when implementing real instrument audio. AI initially proposed several possible storage and implementation approaches. Rather than accepting the first solution, I made a deliberate decision to use the backend database and Cloudinary for audio storage, with the database storing URLs and metadata rather than audio files themselves. I also chose to initially implement real audio for only the Piano while keeping the other instruments synthetic until suitable sources could be identified. This was an example of deliberately scoping down an AI-assisted solution instead of implementing everything at once. Later, the implementation was expanded when suitable sources became available. This approach reduced unnecessary complexity while still leaving the system extensible.

AI also added significant value during debugging and refinement, but these experiences taught me not to assume that every error introduced during development was caused by my latest changes. For example, after modifying the project, I used testing and git stash to verify whether existing test failures were actually caused by my changes. The same four failures occurred even after reverting to the clean baseline, confirming that they were pre-existing and unrelated to my work. This shifted my approach from simply asking AI to “fix the error” to first establishing whether the error was actually something that needed fixing.

Another area where I exercised judgement was maintainability and data consistency. During the badge system development, AI identified a duplication risk caused by badge information being maintained separately in the backend and frontend. Rather than treating the initial implementation as finished, I accepted the underlying concern and changed the architecture by introducing a badge_definitions database table as a single source of truth. This is an example where AI added value not simply by writing code, but by helping expose a design weakness that could have become more problematic as the system grew.

At the same time, I learned that AI-generated content and technical decisions still require verification. For the Guided Music Lessons, some song information could not be reliably obtained from the available sources. Instead of presenting uncertain information as factual, I chose to use illustrative arrangements and explicitly flag estimated BPM values where reliable tempo information could not be found. This reinforced an important limitation of AI-assisted development: an output can look complete and technically plausible while still being based on assumptions or incomplete information.

Overall, my experience showed me that the greatest value of AI was not that it could “build the project for me”, but that it reduced the friction between an idea and a working implementation. It helped me explore solutions quickly, work across unfamiliar parts of the codebase, generate repetitive structures, debug issues and iterate on features. However, I remained responsible for deciding what should actually be built. I rejected suggestions when they did not fit the intended user experience, modified solutions when they introduced unnecessary complexity, and verified outputs when accuracy or system behaviour was uncertain.

The project therefore changed my view of AI-assisted development. I initially saw AI mainly as a tool for generating code, but through repeated iterations I came to see it more as a technical collaborator whose suggestions need to be evaluated rather than followed blindly. The most meaningful skill I developed was not simply learning how to prompt AI effectively, but learning how to recognise when an AI-generated solution was technically possible yet unsuitable for my project's requirements. Going forward, I would continue using AI for rapid prototyping, debugging and exploration, while retaining human judgement over architecture, user experience, accuracy, scope and final implementation decisions.
